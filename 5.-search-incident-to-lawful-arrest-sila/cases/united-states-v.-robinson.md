# United States v. Robinson

## Summary of United States v. Robinson (1973)

### Facts

On April 23, 1968, Officer Jenks stopped Willie Robinson for driving with a revoked license in Washington, D.C. After arresting Robinson, Jenks conducted a full search of his person. During the pat-down, Jenks felt an object in Robinson's coat pocket that he couldn't identify. He removed a crumpled cigarette package, opened it, and discovered 14 gelatin capsules containing heroin. Robinson was convicted of heroin possession and concealment.

### Procedural History

* **District Court**: Convicted Robinson
* **Court of Appeals (D.C. Circuit)**: Reversed, holding the search violated the Fourth Amendment because it exceeded what was necessary for officer safety
* **Supreme Court**: Reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the conviction

### Legal Issue

Whether a police officer may conduct a full search of an arrestee's person incident to a lawful custodial arrest, or whether such searches must be limited to a protective frisk for weapons (as in _Terry v. Ohio_).

### Holding

The Supreme Court held that **a full search of the person incident to a lawful custodial arrest is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment and requires no additional justification beyond the lawful arrest itself**.

### Key Reasoning

**1. Historical Authority**: The Court traced the search-incident-to-arrest doctrine back to _Weeks v. United States_ (1914) and found consistent recognition of the authority to search an arrestee's person in cases like _Agnello v. United States_ (1925) and _Chimel v. California_ (1969).

**2. Dual Justifications**: The authority to search serves two purposes:

* Officer safety (removing weapons)
* Preservation of evidence

**3. Distinction from Terry**: The Court rejected applying _Terry v. Ohio_'s limited frisk standard to custodial arrests, emphasizing that:

* _Terry_ involved stops based on _less than probable cause_
* A custodial arrest is a "wholly different kind of intrusion" than an investigatory stop
* The extended exposure during custody justifies more extensive searches

**4. Bright-Line Rule**: The Court established that officers need not justify each step of a search incident to arrest on a case-by-case basis. The lawful arrest itself provides the authority for a full search—no additional showing of danger or likelihood of finding evidence is required.

**5. Reasonableness**: Once a lawful custodial arrest occurs, a full search of the person is automatically reasonable under the Fourth Amendment.

### Significance

_United States v. Robinson_ established a categorical rule that police may conduct a full search of any person they lawfully arrest, regardless of the offense or perceived danger. This remains a foundational principle in Fourth Amendment search-and-seizure law, creating a bright-line rule that prioritizes officer safety and evidence preservation over individualized assessments of necessity.
